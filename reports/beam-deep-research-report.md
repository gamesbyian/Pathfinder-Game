# Beam-Search Variants with Explicit Diversity

**Diverse Beam Search (DBS):**  This method splits the beam into *G* groups and enforces diversity between them.  At each step DBS adds a dissimilarity term to the score, penalizing candidates that are too similar (e.g. sharing n‑grams or word embeddings) with sequences already chosen in other groups.  In effect, survivors are chosen in each group by a *doubly greedy* process: greedily along time (like standard beam search) and greedily across groups to maximize diversity.  Similarity is measured via user-defined δ functions (Hamming or n‑gram overlap, vector distances).  This is a *local* modification at each step (it does not preserve an external archive), but it uses global context in that groups discourage reusing tokens chosen by earlier groups.  Computational overhead is modest: aside from standard beam expansions, one computes diversity penalties among *B×G* partial sequences (often small beams), plus a constant number of additional comparisons or lookups.  In practice DBS produced significantly more varied outputs with minimal runtime cost.  In image captioning and machine translation tasks, DBS improved the *top-1* output quality by better balancing exploration and exploitation and raised “oracle” metrics (best-of-*$B$ performance) while yielding more diverse lists.  Human evaluations confirmed that DBS captions felt more varied and informative than standard beam results.  DBS tends to help most in ambiguous domains (complex images or dialog) and less in strictly unimodal tasks.  In principle, excessive diversity (too large dissimilarity weight) can hurt the best solution if the model’s top‐score beam is broken apart.  However, Vijayakumar et al. report that DBS often *improves* the best solution because it avoids wasting beam slots on near-duplicates.  Sensitivity to beam width is moderate – DBS still benefits from larger beam sizes (more groups) but authors found its gains “robust over a wide range of values”.  For constrained path construction, the same idea could apply if one defines a novelty measure over partial paths (e.g. path shape or visited features) and groups beams to enforce it. 

**Determinantal Beam Search:**  Meister *et al.* reformulated beam selection as maximizing a determinantal point process (DPP) objective.  At each depth they select the subset of *K* survivors that maximizes the log-determinant of a positive semidefinite kernel matrix, which encodes pairwise similarity (e.g. via string kernels or feature overlap).  In effect, DPP “automatically” penalizes overlapping beams and rewards broad coverage.  This selection is still local per time-step but takes into account interactions among all candidates: it is a global subset optimization at that step.  The similarity metric (kernel) can be chosen to capture domain-specific notions of closeness (n-gram overlap in text; analogous path features in routing).  Overhead is higher: selecting a diverse set requires computing determinants of an *O(K×K)* matrix at each step (plus defining the kernel).  Empirically on machine translation this method produced *much more diverse* sets than plain beam search with only slight BLEU loss.  In fact, it outperformed earlier diversity methods: under a fixed-quality constraint, determinantal beam search gave better diversity trade-offs than both stochastic beam search and Vijayakumar’s DBS.  Because it enforces global (within-beam) variety, it can significantly increase coverage of solution types, though at cost of extra computation.  In practice, results were good in text generation and could transfer to any domain where a similarity kernel over partial states can be defined.  If partial path features (like visited nodes or segment features) can form a kernel, determinantal selection could promote diverse future trajectories in path search. 

**Stochastic Beam Search (Reservoir-like Retention):**  Another strategy is to inject randomness in survivor choice.  Rather than deterministically taking the top-*K* candidates by score, *stochastic beam search* samples *K* beams (without replacement) with probabilities proportional to their heuristic scores.  For example, Clara Meister *et al.* propose **Conditional Poisson Beam Search**, which generalizes beam search into a sampling process.  In CPSBS, each expansion chooses survivors by a conditional Poisson sampling of the candidate distribution, so lower-scoring yet still plausible beams have some chance to survive.  This tends to yield more diverse collections of outputs and provides unbiased estimators of expectations under the model.  Overhead is similar to top-*K* selection but involves a weighted sampling step (e.g. via Gumbel-top-*K* tricks).  In experiments on translation, CPSBS gave *lower-variance estimates* of translation quality and model entropy than previous stochastic methods.  When used as a sampling strategy, it produced more variety without retraining.  However, purely stochastic retention may hurt if one cares only about the absolute best solution – randomness can allow higher-quality beams to drop out.  Thus its benefit is in covering the candidate space; top-1 quality may or may not improve.  Sensitivity to beam width is intuitive: larger beams with sampling yield more exploration.  For constrained path problems, a stochastic retention approach (randomly keeping some lower-scoring states) could help escape greedy traps, but at risk of slower convergence.  *Reservoir sampling* in general (randomly replacing survivors with a fixed probability) is a similar idea: it gives every new state a chance independent of score, boosting diversity at the expense of optimality. 

**Pareto/Multicriteria Beam Selection:**  Instead of a single score, one can equip each beam with a **vector** of objectives (e.g. cost and novelty, or multiple reward signals) and select survivors by Pareto dominance.  For example, a recent proposal “Pareto Beam Search” treats each beam with a vector of scores (e.g. relevance, diversity, freshness) and prunes by non-dominated sorting.  Beams are grouped into Pareto fronts, and selected front-by-front until the beam capacity is filled, using crowding distance to promote spread.  This is a *global, multiobjective* approach: survivors are chosen to cover a Pareto-optimal trade-off surface.  It requires computing Pareto fronts and crowding distances each step, so overhead grows with beam width and number of objectives.  In concept it avoids collapsing to one objective.  Preliminary reports show it can be used as a drop-in replacement for plain beam pruning.  It has been applied in generative retrieval tasks (e.g. codebook generation) and could extend to any search where multiple criteria matter.  It preserves a variety of solutions balanced across objectives.  Downsides: complexity of sorting, and no guarantee on one specific objective.  For path planning, if e.g. distance and novelty are both objectives, Pareto beam search could directly maintain both goals. 

# Exploration-Focused Algorithms

**Novelty Search:**  Novelty search completely eschews a fixed fitness objective and instead rewards behavioral novelty.  In practice, each partial solution (state) is characterized by a set of features (often called a *behavior characterisation*), and its novelty score is the average distance to its *k* nearest neighbors (in a feature space) drawn from an archive of past states and the current population.  Survivor selection then prefers high-novelty individuals.  This is a *global* archive-based method: the archive grows over time with visited behaviors.  The diversity notion is semantic/phenotypic: two states are similar if their characteristic behaviors are close (e.g. positions reached, or trajectory patterns).  Overhead can be substantial: one must compute distances between each candidate and potentially many archived behaviors (though clever data structures or approximate methods can help).  Empirical results (primarily in evolutionary robotics and deceptive optimization) show novelty search can find solutions in problems where fitness-driven search fails.  For example, it has solved deceptive mazes and robot gait tasks.  Curković and Čehulić (2020) found that in complex path planning scenarios, novelty-search–like methods outperformed crowding and fitness-sharing.  Novelty search shines when the objective is misleading or sparse (it finds creative solutions by seeking the unexpected).  However, it can hurt convergence if a true objective exists: since it ignores fitness, it may wander aimlessly or fail to converge to an optimum (unless combined with some objective guidance).  It is sensitive to population size: too small a population or archive may not accurately capture novelty space, while too large slows down computations.  Applying novelty search to constrained path construction could be done by defining novel features of partial paths (like visiting new regions or satisfying different sub-constraints).  But one must choose meaningful features, and for large state spaces, storing an ever-growing archive could be costly.

**Width-Based Search (Iterative Width, Best-First Width Search):**  In AI planning, *width-based* methods explicitly use *state novelty* (often atomic “atoms” or propositions) to guide search.  The classic **Iterative Width (IW)** algorithm assigns a novelty value to each new state as the size of the smallest tuple of propositional features that is true in this state for the first time in the search.  It prunes states whose novelty exceeds a threshold.  Best-First Width Search (BFWS) combines novelty with heuristic distance: for instance, it may rank first by novelty (states with unseen features) and then by heuristic cost.  These notions are *global* in that novelty is measured against all previously visited states (often the frontier or closed list).  The idea is that keeping states with “new facts” leads to exploring different parts of the state space.  Overhead involves tracking seen atoms and checking combinations, but many IW variants use efficient bitsets or incremental checks.  Width-based planners (e.g. IW(1), IW(2)) have had *state-of-the-art* success on many control and Atari-style problems, solving domains traditional heuristics struggled with.  They are especially effective when good heuristics are unavailable but the problem structure yields a low “width”.  However, if the state features yield large widths or if novelty plateaus, these methods revert to brute force.  Larger beam widths (or depth allowances) directly improve IW’s coverage, but overhead grows combinatorially with novelty tuple size.  For a constrained path problem, a width-based approach might treat each partial path’s novelty (e.g. new types of partial solutions) and prioritize those with novel properties.  If constraints are captured by features, IW could systematically drive search into new constraint-satisfaction patterns. 

# Quality-Diversity and Archive-Based Methods

**Quality-Diversity (QD) Optimization (e.g. MAP-Elites, NSLC):**  QD algorithms aim to populate a collection of *diverse high-quality* solutions rather than optimizing a single best.  A hallmark is *MAP-Elites*: one defines a low-dimensional descriptor space (behavioral or structural features) and discretizes it into a grid of cells; each cell stores the best-found individual (“elite”) for that niche.  Survivor selection involves comparing candidates to the current elite in their cell: if better, replace it.  Thus similarity is implicitly defined by the descriptor grid (states close in descriptor fall in same cell).  This is global across the run (an evolving archive of elites).  Computational overhead can be large: managing and evaluating possibly thousands of elites, especially in high-dimensional descriptor spaces.  Nonetheless, QD has yielded impressive results in robotics and design exploration (e.g. discovering a variety of robot gaits, generating resilient controllers).  It systematically maintains coverage of user-defined behavioral dimensions, guaranteeing no niche is empty if reachable.  Diversity hurts only if descriptors poorly capture meaningful variation: then one may fill cells with useless solutions.  The number of cells (and population) must be tuned to cover interesting variation.  For path search, one could choose descriptors like path length and constraint-violation metrics to build a QD grid.  This would explicitly force exploration of different constraint combinations.  However, designing descriptors for partial paths can be tricky, and QD methods usually assume complete solutions (though there is work on “stepping-stone” QD for open-ended searches).

**Archive-Based Selection (Novelty Archive, Elite Archive):**  Some search methods maintain an explicit archive of past states or solutions and use it during selection.  For example, novelty search builds a novelty archive, and multiobjective EAs often keep an external Pareto archive.  Archive selection means survivors are chosen not just by current scores but also by their relation to stored elites.  This can take forms such as “only add a new state to the beam if it’s sufficiently different from all archived states”, or replacing the *least novel* archived solution with the new candidate if it’s novel.  Similarity metrics depend on how the archive is organized (geometric distance in feature space, qualitative matching of constraints, etc.).  These methods are *global* and can greatly increase memory usage.  Empirically, archives help to maintain long-term diversity: e.g. in novelty search, the archive ensures that the search continuously pushes into new territory.  However, archives can slow down (many distance checks) and sometimes bloat with redundant entries.  For path problems, one might archive unique partial paths (abstracted by their last node or uncovered constraints) and avoid re-exploring similar states.

# Niching, Crowding, and Population Diversity

**Niching/Fitness Sharing:**  In evolutionary algorithms, *niching* methods explicitly preserve subpopulations (niches).  **Fitness sharing** lowers the fitness of individuals in dense regions: if two solutions are genetically or phenotypically similar (within some threshold), they share fitness “credit” so neither dominates.  Survivor selection thus naturally favors spreading out: a crowded niche yields smaller fitness.  Similarity is defined by a distance metric (genotype or phenotype).  This is a global scheme each generation.  **Crowding** is another family: e.g. *deterministic crowding* replaces offspring only if they are sufficiently dissimilar to their parents.  These methods have been effective in multimodal optimization, maintaining multiple optima.  The computational cost includes computing pairwise distances or enforcing restricted tournaments, which can be significant for large populations.  Path-planning experiments (Curković & Čehulić) implemented both sharing and crowding in an evolutionary planner.  They found that in moderately complex domains these methods modestly improved exploration, but in very complex scenarios they became *ineffective*: the fitness landscape was so deceptive that genotypic diversity alone did little.  A key failure mode is noted: even if population remains diverse by genotype, the search may still get stuck by deceptive fitness.  In practice, niching helps maintain multiple competing solutions, but if the search objective is inherently misleading, one can converge to many similar local optima.  Niching sensitivity: requires tuning niche radius or sharing factors, and often a large population to fill niches.  For beam search on paths, one could view each beam as an “individual” and apply sharing: e.g. penalize beams with similar endpoints.  Or implement crowding by replacing a beam only if its successor is truly different.  These might help, but the overhead and parameter tuning could be challenging.

**Population Diversity Metrics:**  More generally, population diversity can be quantified by entropy or mean pairwise distance.  Some selection methods explicitly optimize such metrics (e.g. maximizing distance to centroid).  Using a global diversity measure (like the minimal pairwise similarity) one can pick a subset of survivors that maximizes it.  Such schemes (for instance, “max-min diversity”) are essentially special cases of determinantal or multiobjective selection.  They are global (entire population) and expensive to compute (combinatorial subset selection).  In experimental studies, high average pairwise distance often correlates with slower early convergence (since many low-quality solutions are kept), so it must be balanced.  Therefore, purely maximizing a diversity metric without regard to quality often *hurts* the best-solution progress; instead, researchers combine diversity with performance (via multiobjective trade-offs or thresholding). 

# Evaluating Diversity’s Effect

Researchers distinguish “diversity helping search” from mere metric inflation by **performance benchmarks** and **ablation studies**.  A common approach is to compare the search outcome with and without the diversity mechanism while tracking objective performance (best cost found, time to solution, success rate).  For example, Vijayakumar *et al.* showed DBS not only increased a diversity metric but also improved top-1 task scores and oracle metrics over plain beam search.  In quality-diversity contexts, authors observe that exploring diverse behaviors sometimes *enables* finding higher-fitness solutions: e.g. combining diversity led to better Pareto fronts in multi-objective QD.  Curković *et al.* explicitly measured final path quality: novelty-based planning found shorter or safer paths than shared or crowded GA, demonstrating a real benefit.  Conversely, if diversity is only increasing a metric, one might see no change (or a drop) in actual search performance.  Thus, metrics like *coverage* of solution space, number of distinct solutions found, or speed of escaping local optima are tracked.  Typical observables include: **best objective value vs iterations** curves (diverse methods often converge more slowly but avoid plateaus), **success rates** on reaching goals, and **distribution of solutions** found.  In short, researchers look for improvements in end-to-end results (solution quality, coverage of feasible solutions) rather than diversity numbers per se.  If adding diversity only raises an entropy measure but degrades the best solution, it is judged unhelpful.  Many studies therefore report ablations: e.g. turning off diversity penalties should reduce coverage and sometimes worsen the best found, confirming that diversity drove exploration. 

# Comparison of Principles

Based on theory and empirical evidence, the following strategies stand out:

- **Multi-objective / Pareto-based selection:** By treating diversity as a co-objective, Pareto methods naturally balance quality and variety.  The recent Pareto Beam Search exemplifies this.  It has strong support in MOEA research for maintaining diverse fronts.  Its evidence lies in robust concept (avoiding objective bias) and promising early results.  

- **Determinantal/DPP selection:** This provides a principled way to enforce diversity via kernels.  Empirically it outperforms other diversity heuristics in sequence decoding.  Its evidence is high for language tasks (competitive BLEU with more novelty) and it generalizes well if a similarity kernel is available.  

- **Novelty/Width-based search:** These prioritize exploration of *new* regions.  Novelty search is well supported by deceptive-problem experiments.  Width-based methods have strong success in planning benchmarks.  The evidence suggests they solve what greedy search misses.  Their downside is often slower convergence on standard objectives, and heavy dependency on feature design.  Still, novelty-driven search is highly relevant when the standard heuristic is misleading.  

- **Quality-Diversity (QD):** This family (MAP-Elites, NSLC, etc.) is backed by robotics and optimization literature.  It explicitly optimizes for a Pareto of quality vs descriptor coverage.  The evidence shows it finds a spectrum of high-quality solutions and can even aid innovation (e.g. evolving new robot gaits).  Its principle has strong conceptual support, though less applied in path search per se.  

- **Stochastic/Reservoir Sampling:** Randomized beams (CPBS or Gumbel-top-k) have theoretical support for unbiased sampling and practical evidence of reducing variance.  However, they do not guarantee preserving the best score.  Their main evidence is improved estimator accuracy and variety in NLP tasks, but whether that transfers to search-with-constraints is unclear.  

- **Niching/Crowding:** Traditional in GA, these methods are well-established for multimodal optimization.  They have empirical backing in evolutionary path planners.  However, evidence also cautions that simple sharing often fails on very hard problems where the heuristic (fitness) is deceptive.  They are somewhat lower-ranked for pure beam search because they rely on genetic operators and may be less effective without crossover/mutation.  

- **Archive and Abstraction:** Using archives of states or abstract features is often combined with the above (e.g. novelty search’s archive).  While not a standalone selection rule, archives underpin methods like novelty and QD.  Abstracting states (clustering or hashing) is a technique within these methods.  Evidence of their value is indirect: many diversity approaches use abstraction.  

In summary, methods that tie diversity into the core optimization (DPP/Pareto/QD/novelty) have the strongest evidence of truly enhancing search.  Simple diversity metrics or random retention can help but carry a risk of diluting quality.  The most relevant principles for a constrained path beam search are likely **novelty-driven selection** (if a meaningful novelty measure exists) and **Pareto/multiobjective selection** (to trade off path quality vs exploration).  Determinantal point-process selection is also promising if one can define a kernel over partial paths.  Quality-diversity could be adapted by defining path descriptors.  Niching methods may help moderate cases but are less flexible when no breeding is done.  Ultimately, the best approach depends on the domain’s structure; existing results suggest that purely focusing on score often misses valid solutions, so methods explicitly preserving a variety of plausible futures tend to perform better.
