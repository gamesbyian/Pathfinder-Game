# Pathfinder Solver: Literature & Practice Review

**TL;DR**

- The persistent‑3‑level failure pattern is *two distinct pathologies in one codebase*: L92/L134 are explorer‑never‑gets‑close failures (heuristic plateau on must‑pass / reqInt obligations) and L108 is a final‑mile flooding failure; the literature treats these as different problems with different remedies, and the existing "explorer + closer" architecture is by construction incapable of fixing L92.
- The 130‑level mass‑regression class on telemetry/canonicalization changes is a known engineering anti‑pattern (mutating identity / equality of a "canonical result" struct used as a cache or comparison key); the planning‑solver community solves it by *side‑channel telemetry* (Fast Downward style: per‑instance log files keyed by `(domain, problem, run‑id)` outside the search‑node struct), and bound‑correctness fixes are landed via the "replay every solved instance's known good plan and assert no prune fires" pattern (a first cousin of CDCL trail replay; in the IPC literature this is called *plan‑validation guarded refactoring*).
- The single highest‑leverage two‑week investment is *G1+H1+F2*: instrument the 137 hand‑verified hint paths as a per‑state oracle (h‑plot, prune‑fire counterexample log, separating‑function red‑team), add a side‑channel telemetry sink that does not touch canonicalization, and use those two together to land the must‑pass urgency sign fix and a Held‑Karp landmark heuristic without further mass regressions.

## A. Heuristic plateau on must-pass / must-cross obligations (the L92 family)

### A1. Sign convention for must-pass urgency

In the classical‑planning and heuristic‑search literature, an *urgency‑to‑satisfy‑a‑mandatory‑fact* term is essentially a landmark/goal‑count residual: it is the count (or weighted count) of unsatisfied landmarks that must still be achieved on every plan suffix. The canonical convention (Richter & Westphal, *The LAMA Planner: Guiding Cost‑Based Anytime Planning with Landmarks*, JAIR 2010, vol. 39, pp. 127–177; Richter, Helmert & Westphal, AAAI 2008) is:

- **Sign:** for an *additive* h that the search *minimizes*, the term is non‑negative and added with a positive coefficient — more unmet landmarks ⇒ higher h ⇒ less attractive. Equivalently, when scoring a state for *maximization* (your beam's `score` driver), the term must enter with a **negative** coefficient so that more unmet must‑pass tiles ⇒ lower score.
- **Magnitude:** LAMA uses unit per landmark by default and re‑weights only via a separate cost‑sensitive term; [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220543191_The_LAMA_Planner_Guiding_Cost-Based_Anytime_Planning_with_Landmarks) Richter & Westphal's "cost vs coverage debate" (§5–6 of JAIR 2010) explicitly warns that aggressive cost weighting can hurt coverage — the point that a *purely counting* landmark term, with consistent positive sign relative to "fewer landmarks is better," is the safest default.

So in your driver, with *additive‑maximization* semantics (`score = α·lengthPressure + β·mustPassUrgency + …`), the *intended* sign of `mustPassUrgency` is **negative when measuring "still‑remaining must‑pass" and the multiplier β is positive**, OR the urgency is computed as a non‑negative *bonus for having visited* and β is positive. The L92 signature `mustPassUrgency = −36.52, lengthPressure = 107` indicates the term is firing with a strongly negative value while remaining must‑pass tiles exist — this is consistent with "remaining count" entering with a positive sign and β positive, which *pushes the search away* from must‑pass tiles. That is almost certainly an inverted gradient, not an intentional ε‑decoy.

When is *negative urgency* (push away) ever intentional? Only in two narrow cases in the literature:

1. **Decoy / false‑goal avoidance** (Hoffmann's "local search topology" line of work; Hoffmann, JAIR 2005, *Where 'Ignoring Delete Lists' Works*): a state that *appears* to be a landmark but is a trap can be assigned a small negative push; this is an *ε‑decoy*, magnitude ≪ the true landmark term. A magnitude of 36 in a 100‑scale driver is not ε‑decoy magnitude.
2. **Repulsion from already‑satisfied landmarks** to avoid revisiting (LAMA's "accepted landmarks" set). But that pushes away from *satisfied* landmarks, not unsatisfied ones.

**Test without re‑deriving the driver.** Two small, low‑risk diagnostics, both standard in solver engineering:

- **Monotonicity probe along the hint path** (see G1 below): for each prefix of the hand‑verified hint path, log `mustPassUrgency`. If the term *increases* as the path approaches the next required must‑pass tile (rather than decreasing toward 0 at the moment of visit), the gradient is inverted. This is the planning analogue of the "h‑plot along π\\*" test recommended in Richter & Westphal (2010 §7) and is the cheapest possible verification.
- **Sign‑flip A/B**: ship a feature flag `mustPassUrgencySign ∈ {+1,−1,0}` and run the audit at all three settings on a sample of L92‑family levels. If +1 vs −1 reverses the failure, the bug is the sign; if both fail and 0 (term off) succeeds, the term is mis‑scaled, not mis‑signed.

### A2. Held‑Karp waypoint heuristic operational details

The Held‑Karp DP for TSP has time `O(n²·2ⁿ)` [Computer Geek +2](https://compgeek.co.in/held-karp-algorithm-for-tsp/) and is well known to give an admissible lower bound on tour cost for ≤~20 waypoints (Held & Karp, *J. SIAM* 1962; see also Approximating the Held‑Karp Bound, arXiv:1702.04307 / 2411.14745). For your `≤10` waypoints in‑browser, it costs ~`100·1024 ≈ 10⁵` table cells per state, which is feasible *if precomputed once per (level, portal‑automaton state)* — see (b).

(a) **Required self‑intersections (reqInt) as ordered virtual waypoints.** Treating each required intersection as an additional ordered waypoint *does* preserve admissibility *only if* you (i) require any tour through `Waypoints ∪ IntersectionEvents` to traverse each intersection event at the position dictated by the temporal ordering of the actual self‑intersections, and (ii) use a *path‑variant* (s‑t Path TSP) Held‑Karp lower bound rather than the cyclic version, since you have a fixed gate (start) and goal (end). The Path‑TSP variant of Held‑Karp is admissible (An et al., *Improving Christofides' Algorithm for the s‑t Path TSP*, arXiv:1110.4604; Traub & Vygen). [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1110.4604) The naive composition double‑counts whenever the same lattice edge serves both a must‑cross obligation and an intersection event: the must‑cross edge is counted in the path cost and the intersection event re‑adds the same step. **Correction:** treat intersection events not as distinct waypoints but as a *constraint on the multiset of edges visited* (degree‑≥2 constraint on at least k vertices), and add only `k` (the count) — *not* a TSP tour through the intersection vertices — to the lower bound. This is admissible because each self‑intersection costs at least one extra step beyond the underlying simple path. Equivalently: `h_lb = HK(Waypoints, geodesic_d) + reqInt_remaining`, with the `+reqInt_remaining` an additive admissible "extra cost of self‑intersections" term.

(b) **Precompute APSP per (level, portal‑automaton state) once.** Yes, this is the right granularity and it is the engineering choice taken by all symbolic planners that handle nondeterministic operators (e.g., merge‑and‑shrink with abstract state spaces, Helmert et al., JAIR 2014). Concretely: enumerate the portal‑automaton states (small — typically ≤ 2^(#colors) commitment masks); for each, run Floyd‑Warshall on the (≤144)‑cell grid restricted to edges legal in that automaton state; cache the (cell, cell) distance table. Each table is ≤ `144·144·1 byte ≈ 20 KB`, so even 16 portal states is `< 400 KB` — fine for a browser. *Do not* recompute per frontier node — that is the asymptotic blow‑up to avoid.

(c) **Combining LAMA‑counting with metric tour bound, monotonically along the known‑good path.** The LAMA approach of *separate queues per heuristic* (Richter & Westphal 2010 §4; Helmert, JAIR 2006, *The Fast Downward Planning System*) is the safest engineering — keep `landmarks_unmet` as one ordering and `f = g + HK_bound + manhattan` as a second, alternate between them. If you *must* combine into a single scalar `h(s) = α·landmarks_unmet(s) + β·HK_bound(s) + γ·manhattan(s)`, monotonicity along π\\* requires:

- α, β, γ ≥ 0;
- `landmarks_unmet` strictly decreases at every landmark visit and is constant otherwise (it is by construction; the LAMA pseudo‑heuristic is monotone non‑increasing along any plan that achieves landmarks in any order — see Richter & Westphal §3.3);
- `HK_bound` is recomputed as a *path‑TSP through remaining waypoints starting from the current cell*, not the original gate — this is the key detail and the failure mode if you precompute once. Re‑rooting the HK bound at the current cell is `O(2^k·k)` per evaluation but `k ≤ 10` so it's ~10⁴ ops — fine.

The Richter & Westphal "cost vs coverage" debate (JAIR 2010 §7, Tables 8–10) concluded empirically that *cost‑sensitivity* (weighting α by action cost) hurt coverage in many domains and they recommend the *uniform‑cost LAMA heuristic plus separate cost‑sensitive WA\\* layer* — the planning analogue of "don't fold action cost into the count, keep them in separate ordering layers."

### A3. Counting must-cross edges as landmarks

Edge‑landmarks are not native to delete‑relaxation: `h+`/`h_max`/`h_FF` operate on facts (vertex/proposition landmarks), not edges. The standard transformation, used in PDDL encodings of edge‑traversal problems and in MAPF benchmarks, is:

- **Subdivision / dummy‑vertex:** for each must‑cross edge `(u,v)`, insert a degree‑2 dummy vertex `m_{uv}` and replace the edge with two edges `(u, m_{uv}), (m_{uv}, v)`; declare `m_{uv}` a fact landmark. Now LAMA‑style landmark counting captures the must‑cross obligation. This is sound but has two caveats:
  - **reqLen:** subdivision changes path length by `+1` per must‑cross edge. If you do this transformation, `reqLen` must be adjusted by `+|mustCross|`. Failure to adjust silently shifts every reqLen check by a constant — a classic "unit conversion" bug.
  - **reqInt:** intersection counting must operate on the *original* lattice, not the subdivided one (otherwise dummy vertices generate spurious self‑intersections at degree‑2). Keep a parallel "logical position" alongside the subdivided "search position."
- **Alternative without transformation:** a *disjunctive action landmark* set `{a | a traverses (u,v)}` is an action‑landmark in the sense of Helmert & Domshlak (ICAPS 2009, *Landmarks, Critical Paths and Abstractions*) and integrates into LM‑cut. This avoids reqLen/reqInt distortion entirely, at the cost of more elaborate landmark machinery. For a 15K‑LOC JS solver, the dummy‑vertex transformation is the minimum‑viable choice.

### A4. Schedule‑aware feasibility for joint must‑pass + reqInt

The cleanest published joint feasibility lower bound combines:

- (a) `reqInt_remaining` (extra steps from required intersections, admissible because each intersection adds ≥1 step beyond a simple path);
- (b) `path_TSP_lb({must‑pass remaining} ∪ {must‑cross dummies}, current cell, goal)` using Held‑Karp s‑t variant under the current portal‑automaton state's distance table;
- (c) implicitly subsumed by (b);
- (d) implicitly subsumed by (b) via the right APSP table.

The literature‑grounded admissible combination is **the maximum, not the sum** of individually admissible terms — this avoids over‑pruning. A naive sum over‑prunes whenever a single edge satisfies multiple obligations (e.g., the geodesic to the goal *passes through* a must‑pass tile that is also a portal commitment cell). The Held‑Karp tour bound already accounts for joint visiting, so the safe formulation is:

```
h_admissible(s) = max( reqInt_remaining(s),
                       HK_path_lb(W(s), pos(s), goal | automaton(s)),
                       reqLen − g(s) )    [must reach reqLen exactly]
```

The "intersection slack" / Lagrangian correction is the standard remedy when summing is needed for guidance: maintain `slack = remaining_steps − HK_lb − reqInt_remaining`; if slack < 0, infeasible (prune). This is the planning analogue of the *resource‑variable Cartesian abstraction* used in Fast Downward Aidos / Scorpion (Seipp et al., Unsolvability IPC 2016 abstracts; [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/3171837.3172031) [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/280654834_Counterexample-Guided_Cartesian_Abstraction_Refinement_for_Classical_Planning) Seipp, Keller & Helmert, JAIR 2020 *Saturated Cost Partitioning*) — exactly the cleanest known correction.

The L92 numbers — `lb floor 18..24`, `nearStates = 0`, `mustPassUrgency = −36.52` — match the signature of an inverted‑gradient + naive‑sum lower bound that *is* admissible (lb is real) but the *driver* steers away from must‑pass cells, so the search cannot reduce that lb. The fix is A1 (sign) + this max‑combination, in that order.

## B. Final-mile / closure flooding (the L108 family)

### B1. 15,000 states one move from a valid solution

(a) The literature consensus is that *near‑boundary saturation* on constraint‑rich problems is **heuristic insensitivity in the closure neighborhood combined with tiebreaking degeneracy**, not soft‑prune softness. The diagnostic taxonomy:

- **Heuristic insensitivity (most common):** `h(s)` and `h(s')` are equal (both 1) for many `s,s'` adjacent to a solution; the heuristic does not distinguish a state where the *unsatisfied obligation can still be satisfied* from one where it cannot. Cohen & Beck (AAAI 2018, *Fat‑ and Heavy‑Tailed Behavior in Satisficing Planning*; SoCS 2017) showed this is the dominant cause of GBFS plateaus, and the depth of the deepest local minimum correlates exponentially with search effort. [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Understanding-the-Search-Behaviour-of-Greedy-Search-Heusner-Keller/ea1c47ddb9df5f2bf57bccb7b7a6a08ba478b900)
- **Tiebreaking degeneracy:** all states have identical `(f, g, h)` tuples; the search expands them in arbitrary order. Heusner, Keller & Helmert (ICAPS 2017, *Understanding the Search Behaviour of Greedy Best‑First Search*) showed this is provable on contrived instances and observed on real ones.
- **Soft‑prune soft enough but irrelevant:** prunes that fire on truly hopeless states are usually fine; the issue is that the *frontier* contains too many *not‑hopeless* states.

**Diagnostic to distinguish:** sample 200 of the `nearSolutionStates` and compute the histogram of `(h, secondary‑h, g, action‑set)`. If `h` is constant across all 200, you have heuristic insensitivity (case 1). If `h` varies but `(h, g)` ties, you have tiebreaking degeneracy (case 2). For L108, the audit shows `nearSolutionStates ≈ 2,500–15,000` with `lb = 1` always — that is the unmistakable signature of *heuristic insensitivity* (case 1): every one of those states has the same `h = 1` and the search has no gradient.

(b) **Best secondary cost on FOCAL for length+count constraints.** In the Pearl & Kim 1982 → Thayer & Ruml 2008/2011 → Cohen, Greco & Ma 2018 (IJCAI, *Anytime Focal Search with Applications*) line, the FOCAL secondary that empirically performs best on combined‑constraint problems is the **distance‑to‑go estimate `d(n)`** (Thayer, Ruml & Kreis SoCS 2009, *Using Distance Estimates in Heuristic Search*) [Unh](https://www.cs.unh.edu/~ruml/papers/rrd-aaai-2022.pdf) — *not* `h(n)` itself, which is the cost‑to‑go. For your problem, `d(n)` is the *number of moves* still required, regardless of "score": `d(n) = max(reqLen − g(n), HK_steps_lb)`. Thayer–Ruml's EES (IJCAI 2011, *Bounded Suboptimal Search: A Direct Approach Using Inadmissible Estimates*) [Unh](https://www.cs.unh.edu/~ruml/papers/ios-socs19.pdf) uses `(f̂, ĥ, d̂)` triples and outperforms WA\\* substantially when `h` is uninformative near the goal. Specifically, the *expansion delay* / `xe(n) + Tf(n)` rule from CDXES (Ruml's group, AAAI 2022, *New Results in Bounded‑Suboptimal Search*) is the recent state‑of‑the‑art for breaking final‑mile floods. The two‑week practical recommendation: a single FOCAL list ordered by `d̂(n) − violations_satisfiable(n)` where the latter is a count of obligations *that can still be satisfied within the remaining length budget*. For L108 specifically, this is exactly the "completable‑predicate" approach Stevens, Bulitko & Sturtevant used to accelerate Witness‑puzzle search 6× (arXiv:2308.02666, *Solving Witness‑type Triangle Puzzles Faster with an Automatically Learned Human‑Explainable Predicate*) — directly applicable to your domain since Witness is the same path‑on‑grid‑with‑constraints class.

### B2. Bounded-completion BFS as closer

(a) **Branching‑factor budget on 12×12 with portals (b ≈ 6–8).** In single‑threaded JS at ~10M ops/sec and ~1000 ops per state expansion (state copy, hash, prune checks), you get ~10⁴ expansions per 1‑sec frame budget, or ~10⁵ over a 10‑sec interactive timeout. With `b = 8`, depth `d` gives `8^d` nodes: `d=4 → 4096`, `d=5 → 32K`, `d=6 → 262K`, `d=7 → 2M`. **Defensible upper bound: k ≤ 6**, possibly 7 with aggressive duplicate detection (which 12×12 has — most states revisit cells). Beyond k=7, BFS is intractable in a JS browser frame.

(b) **Weighted‑BFS vs pure BFS for reqLen problems.** Yes — Thayer & Ruml SoCS 2009 (above) and the broader bounded‑suboptimal literature show that any informative `h` term breaks symmetry and dominates pure BFS empirically by ~10× nodes expanded. For reqLen specifically, `h_remaining = max(0, reqLen − g − geodesic_to_goal)` is admissible (you must spend at least geodesic_to_goal more steps and reach reqLen total) and dominates BFS within k≤6.

(c) **Caching closer's expanded states across frontier seeds.** *Soundness depends on whether the closer's prune criteria depend on the path history vs only on the current state*. For your problem, several constraints are inherently path‑global: `reqInt` (count of self‑intersections so far), `reqLen` (steps so far), portal‑automaton commitment state. The state for caching MUST therefore be `(cell, automaton_state, must_pass_satisfied_bitmask, must_cross_satisfied_bitmask, intersections_so_far, steps_so_far)`, not just `cell`. With this projection, caching is sound — this is exactly the merge‑and‑shrink "abstract state" formulation (Helmert, Haslum, Hoffmann & Nissim, JAIR 2014). Caching on `cell` alone is unsound: portal commitments make the same cell reachable under different sub‑states with different completion possibilities.

### B3. Meet-in-the-middle with portal automaton

Holte, Felner, Sharon, Sturtevant & Chen, *MM: A bidirectional search algorithm that is guaranteed to meet in the middle* (AAAI 2016 / AIJ 2017, vol. 252, pp. 232–266) [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/MM:-A-bidirectional-search-algorithm-that-is-to-in-Holte-Felner/b57038f7126a6952c411c4e8c826816755d53b98) [Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.du.edu/computer_science_faculty/10/) is the modern reference. Key constraints for your setup:

(a) **Backward operator definition with non‑deterministic forward operator.** When `enter(portal_color)` toggles commitment substate, the backward operator is `pre‑image of enter`: from a state `(cell, commitment=on)` the predecessors include `(any cell of that portal color, commitment=off) + applying enter`. This is well‑defined as a *set‑valued* backward operator (multiple predecessors per state), which is the standard formulation in regression planning (Rintanen, *Regression for Classical and Nondeterministic Planning*, ECAI 2008; Torralba, *Revisiting Regression in Planning*, IJCAI 2013). The MM theorem (Holte et al. 2017, Theorem 2) requires only that the backward operator's pre‑image be enumerable; non‑determinism in the forward direction means the *backward branching factor* increases (you have ≥2 predecessors per `enter`‑terminating state) but soundness is preserved. A subtle gotcha: ensure the backward search uses the *same* automaton‑aware APSP table as A2(b), or it may visit cells unreachable in the actual forward direction.

(b) **High backward branching factor.** This is the textbook regime where MM is *not* the right tool — the AIJ 2017 paper (§7, "When MM helps") explicitly states that MM's advantage shrinks when forward and backward branching factors are highly asymmetric. The newer **BAE\\*** family (Sadhukhan 2013; [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.10272) Shaham, Felner, Sturtevant & Rosenschein, IJCAI 2023, *Front‑to‑End Bidirectional Heuristic Search with Consistent Heuristics*; Siag et al. *On Parallel External‑Memory Bidirectional Search*, [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319232036_MM_A_bidirectional_search_algorithm_that_is_guaranteed_to_meet_in_the_middle) arXiv:2412.21104; *Bidirectional Bounded‑Suboptimal Heuristic Search with Consistent Heuristics*, arXiv:2511.10272) prioritizes nodes by `b(n) = g(n) + h(n) + d(n)` where `d` is a heuristic‑error lower bound — this naturally adapts to asymmetric branching by pushing expansions onto the side with tighter h. **PEM‑BAE\\*** (Siag et al. 2024) explicitly outperforms MM on asymmetric problems. For your case: BAE\\* (or its weighted variant WBAE\\*) is the right substitute. **Implementation effort:** roughly equivalent to MM (both need a backward operator, both need two priority queues with an ordering function); BAE\\* needs an additional `d(n)` estimate which for grid‑with‑portals is simply the *Manhattan distance ignoring portals* used as a lower bound on forward heuristic error. ~2–3 engineer‑days delta vs MM if MM is already specified. For browser‑JS, both are feasible; the dominant cost is maintaining the two open lists with `O(log n)` priority queue operations, which is ~`1µs` per op in V8 — fine.

## C. Rescue gates: observability and gate-firing diagnosis

### C1. Predicate failure analysis for nearClosureRescue conjunction

The standard solver‑engineering pattern is **(a) first** — log per‑predicate truth value across all attempts, identify the short‑circuiting predicate. This is the planning analogue of CDCL's *implication graph dump* and is universal good practice; you already have `nearSolutionStates ≈ 3000+` and `lb = 1`, so two of the five predicates clearly evaluate true; the question is *which of the remaining three* is silently false. Without that log, every "fix" is guessing.

After the per‑predicate log identifies the short‑circuit, the choice between (b) ladder predicates and (c) sliding‑window k‑of‑n is informed by the SAT/CP nogood/restart‑policy literature:

- **Audemard & Simon, *Refining Restarts Strategies for SAT and UNSAT*, CP 2012** and **Biere & Fröhlich, *Evaluating CDCL Restart Schemes*** both argue that *threshold predicates that depend on a single global counter* are fragile because the counter's relationship to the underlying phenomenon is rarely linear. Their recommended replacement is **sliding‑window relative measures**, not absolute ones — e.g., LBD glucose restarts trigger when "average LBD in last 50 conflicts > global average × factor." The structural analogue for your gate is option (c): "≥3 of 5 sub‑predicates true on ≥3 of last 4 attempts." This is more robust because it is *resilient to the failure of any single proxy* — exactly the failure mode you have.
- **Steinmetz & Hoffmann, *State Space Search Nogood Learning*, AIJ 2017, vol. 245, pp. 1–37** [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Search-and-Learn:-On-Dead-End-Detectors,-the-Traps-Steinmetz-Hoffmann/e611032583d1db800e2da2ca29a81a81190c184e) further argue that conjunction‑threshold gates should be *learned* (refined online based on observed mismatches between gate prediction and outcome) rather than hand‑weighted. For a 14‑attempt, 137‑level setting that is overkill; the static k‑of‑n window is the right tool.

**Verdict:** do (a) first (1 day of work, must), then (c) (sliding window k‑of‑n) over (b) (ladder); (b) creates a combinatorial test matrix you cannot afford on 14 attempts.

### C2. "Frontier counter never reaches threshold"

The principled fix from the SAT/CP literature is **replace the underlying counter with one that correlates with the failure mode** — option 3 of your three. Lowering thresholds (option 1) is a band‑aid that introduces false positives elsewhere; derivative/stall predicates (option 2) are good *if the counter does correlate but slowly*, but on L92 the audit shows `frontier counter` is a *categorically wrong* proxy (the failure happens with a healthy frontier).

Steinmetz & Hoffmann (AIJ 2017) provide the published methodology for "gate counter is wrong proxy": maintain a *misclassification log* of (gate fired, outcome) and (gate not fired, outcome) tuples; if false‑negative rate on the failure class exceeds 50% across N≥10 attempts, the counter is definitively the wrong proxy and must be replaced — they describe this as "online refinement of dead‑end detectors." [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361555466_Towards_Clause-Learning_State_Space_Search_Learning_to_Recognize_Dead-Ends) The published heuristic for picking a replacement counter is to choose one *defined on a structurally different feature* (cell‑level rather than frontier‑level, or obligation‑level rather than state‑level). For L92 specifically, the right replacement counter is **obligation‑reduction slope over a sliding window** — your audit already shows `0.0025–0.005` on hard levels vs `0.02–2.3` on solved ones, a ~10× separation. A predicate like "obligation‑reduction slope < 0.01 over last 200K expansions" *correlates with the failure mode* on the existing telemetry, which is the criterion Steinmetz & Hoffmann recommend.

## D. Browser-only deployment constraints

### D1. Practical CP/SMT in the browser, 2026

(a) Three options exist, and based on size/perf requirements:

- **z3‑solver (npm `z3-solver`, Microsoft, MIT‑licensed, ~10–15 MB WASM payload):** The npm package is actively maintained (4.16.0, last published 3 months [npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/z3-solver) before May 2026 per npmjs.com listing; Microsoft Z3guide JS bindings documented). Pit‑Claudel's z3.wasm benchmark notes *load* time of ~15 sec on Chrome / <1 sec on Firefox, with verification within 2–5× of native performance. [GitHub](https://github.com/cpitclaudel/z3.wasm) **Verdict:** loads slowly, exceeds 5 MB by 2–3×, but per‑query performance is acceptable; it requires SharedArrayBuffer (cross‑origin isolation headers) for threading. [npm](https://www.npmjs.com/package/z3-solver) Not a fit for your "ship to ≤5 MB" constraint without aggressive trimming.
- **MiniZinc‑js (`minizinc` npm package, MiniZinc/minizinc‑js):** WASM build of the full MiniZinc compiler + Gecode/Chuffed/HiGHS/CBC vendored. [GitHub](https://github.com/MiniZinc/minizinc-js) The build artifact is large (>20 MB across solvers); useful as a NodeJS server‑side tool but exceeds browser payload constraints. For shipping a game it is impractical.
- **clingo‑wasm (`clingo-wasm`, Domoritz, MIT, ~3–5 MB WASM):** The smallest viable option. Used in shipped browser apps (e.g., the IcoSoKu solver, github.com/nrizzo/3coSoKu uses clingo‑wasm to solve in‑browser puzzles, [GitHub](https://github.com/nrizzo/3coSoKu) demonstrating the deployment pattern). For ~50‑cell × 10‑obligation × portal‑automaton problems, ASP encoding gives reliable sub‑second performance once the WASM module loads (~1 sec cold start). **Verdict:** clingo‑wasm at ~3 MB is the only realistic in‑browser CP option for your size budget, *if* you frame the precompute as ASP.

For ~100 ms class performance on 50‑cell × 10‑obligation × portal‑automaton problems: I find no published benchmark that hits this target reliably from a cold WASM load. Warm‑start (after ~1 sec init) typical Z3 in‑browser query times for problems of this size are 10–500 ms based on the z3.wasm and Z3guide JS Sudoku examples. **Honest answer: ~100 ms is not consistently achievable; expect a heavy‑tailed 50–2000 ms distribution.**

(b) **Hybrid deployment for 137‑level shipped game.** This is the textbook *ship offline, solve online* pattern; equivalent to the chess‑opening‑book pattern. The blueprint:

- Pre‑solve all 137 levels offline; ship `hint_paths.json` with the 137 packed coordinate sequences.
- For partial‑state hints from the user's cursor, JS solver finds the *shortest completion* from cursor to nearest hint‑path waypoint (this is a small, fast search — typically <10 ms with no portal complexity in the local neighborhood).

**Compression ratio for 137 path solutions:** each path is at most 144 cells × 1 byte (cell index packing) ≈ 144 B raw, average ~50–100 cells. With gzip on JSON of packed coords: ~30–60 B per path average, total ~6–10 KB compressed for all 137 levels. This is negligible (<1% of any reasonable game asset budget). The published precedent for this pattern in pencil‑and‑paper puzzle game shipping is e.g. *The Witness* (Thekla, Inc.) which ships pre‑validated puzzle solutions; the academic analogue is Witness‑puzzle solver work (Stevens et al. 2023, arXiv:2308.02666; Iwashita et al. 2012 for path enumeration on Witness‑grids).

### D2. Trap/nogood learning in pure JS without native bitset

For ~12‑bit obligation bitmasks, a packed JS Number (which is a 32‑bit integer for bitwise ops) is the right primitive — *do not* use `Set<number>` directly as the leaf container. Recommended structure:

```
Map<key32: number, leafBitmask: number>
```

where `key32` packs `(tile_x, tile_y, automaton_state)` into a single 32‑bit int and `leafBitmask` is the obligations‑satisfied bitmask. This is `O(1)` lookup, and `leafBitmask` operations are single‑cycle. **Map<number, Set<number>> is 2–3× slower** because Set has per‑element pointer overhead and triggers minor GC.

For larger projections, **lemire/TypedFastBitSet.js** (npm `typedfastbitset`) is the published reference: Uint32Array‑backed BitSet, benchmarked at 60–80 ops/sec for whole‑bitmap union over realistic sizes vs 2.6 ops/sec for native `Set` (per its own benchmark). [GitHub](https://github.com/lemire/TypedFastBitSet.js/) For trap learning at scale on 12‑bit obligation masks, `Map<number, number>` keyed on 32‑bit packed projection is sufficient and stays under GC pressure thresholds for the typical ~10⁵ entries you'd accumulate per attempt.

**Threshold above which GC overhead negates savings.** No published planning‑specific number exists, but two engineering observations from the JS bitset benchmark literature and from solver folklore:

- `Map<number, …>` with > ~10⁶ entries triggers V8's old‑generation GC pauses in the 50–200 ms range, comparable to a typical solver budget. Stay under ~5×10⁵ trap entries per attempt.
- If you cross ~10⁶, switch to a *single Uint32Array indexed by packed projection* — flat array lookups are 3–10× faster than Map and have zero GC pressure. The break‑even is around ~10⁵ entries (at which point you're paying for the array allocation but winning on every lookup).

For your 12‑bit obligation mask × 144 cells × ~16 portal states ≈ 2.4×10⁵ projection space: a flat Uint32Array of size 2^20 (~4 MB) keyed by a 20‑bit projection is the cleanest implementation and gives O(1) access with no GC.

## E. Per-level adaptive policy without overfitting

### E1. Algorithm-selection with N=134 solved + N=3 hard

(a) **Regularization with N=134, 30–50 features.** This is the small‑sample, high‑dimensional regime where overfitting is essentially guaranteed for any model with >~20 effective parameters. Empirical findings from the algorithm‑selection literature:

- **SATzilla'11 (Xu, Hutter, Hoos & Leyton‑Brown, JAIR 2008 vol. 32 pp. 565–606; SATzilla2012, Xu et al. SAT 2012):** uses *cost‑sensitive pairwise classifiers* (one classifier per pair of solvers, predicting which wins) with random forests of shallow trees as the default. They explicitly note that for portfolio sizes <10 and instance counts <500, *hand‑curated decision lists outperform learned models* in cross‑validation in many ASlib scenarios.
- **AutoFolio (Lindauer, Hoos, Hutter & Schaub, JAIR 2015, vol. 53 pp. 745–778; IJCAI 2017 extended abstract):** explicitly reports that for the smaller ASlib scenarios, the gain from learning over a sensible single‑best‑solver baseline is small and easily reversed by feature‑selection variance. **Their default for low‑data scenarios is L1‑regularized regression with feature selection plus a fallback‑to‑single‑best policy.**
- **Kerschke et al. (2019), *Automated Algorithm Selection: Survey and Perspectives*, ECJ:** state that with N<200 instances, "the additional value of selection over a well‑chosen single solver is rarely statistically significant" and recommend portfolios of ≤4 well‑decorrelated solvers with hand‑weighted features.

**Most robust against overfitting in your regime:** *hand‑curated decision list* with ≤5 binary features (e.g., `reqInt > 5`, `mustCross > 0`, `portals ≥ 2`, `density(blocks) > 0.3`, `reqLen > 0.5·area`). Random forests are second‑best but require holdout discipline you can't really afford with N=134.

(b) **Portfolio diversity guidance.** SATzilla papers don't give an explicit minimum diversity number, but two metrics from the AS literature are operational:

- **Marginal contribution / VBS gap:** each policy in the portfolio should solve at least one instance that no other policy solves, OR contribute >5% to the virtual‑best‑solver coverage. AutoFolio papers explicitly compute marginal contribution and prune policies below threshold.
- **Pairwise correlation of solve‑times:** policies with Pearson correlation >0.8 on solve times across the training set are essentially redundant; AS competitions (Lindauer et al. 2018, *The Algorithm Selection Competitions 2015 and 2017*) routinely deduplicate at this threshold.

For your 5–8 named policies (`harvestThenFinish, nearClosureRescue, mustCrossFirst, portalCommitted, knotBuilder`), *measure* the pairwise correlation matrix on the 134 solved levels first. If two policies correlate >0.8, merge them — that's likely the case for `harvestThenFinish` vs `mustCrossFirst` since both prioritize obligation harvest.

(c) **Right answer for the 3 hard, label‑less, OOD levels.** All three options you list have published precedent, but the consensus from SATzilla'12 (Xu et al., SAT competition descriptions) and AutoFolio (Lindauer et al. JAIR 2015 [GitHub](https://github.com/mlindauer/AutoFolio) §6) is **route to "fallback to closer‑stage and exact micro‑solver" track**. The reasoning: a recommender with no training signal on these cases will overfit to the closest‑seen‑instance feature pattern, which is exactly the failure mode (a SATzilla pathology called "presolver overconfidence"). The principled fallback is the *backup solver*: a SAT/CP exact solve on a tractable abstraction. This is also what Hapori Stone Soup (IPC 2023 portfolio winner) does for the unsolved tail — *route to the most aggressive‑memory exact track*, not to the recommender's best guess.

### E2. Per-level pass-order seeding without learned model

The IPC and classical‑planning community has a documented standard for this: it is called a **portfolio configuration script** or **Stone Soup**‑style schedule. The Stone Soup pattern (Helmert, Röger & Karpas, IPC 2011 abstracts; Seipp et al., Hapori Stone Soup planner abstracts IPC 2023): [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/3171837.3172031)

- Run a *fixed sequence* of (planner, time‑budget) pairs, hand‑picked offline based on per‑domain performance on training set.
- The IPC explicitly *allows* hand‑tuned per‑domain configurations; what is forbidden post‑feature‑stop is *changing them based on test‑set performance* (IPC 2023 ipc2023‑classical.github.io rules: "After the feature stop deadline, we allow competitors to send only pull requests with bug fixes... no big changes or parameter tuning"). [Ipc2023-learning](https://ipc2023-learning.github.io/) The discipline is *commit‑then‑freeze*.
- Seipp's Fast Downward Stone Soup demonstrates the pattern with ~15 hand‑tuned (heuristic, search algorithm) pairs.

**How IPC winners avoid "tuning on failing 3 regresses on other 134":** the published discipline is twofold:

1. *Cross‑validate*: tune on 80% of training set, evaluate on held‑out 20%. With N=137 and 3 hard, you don't have the luxury, so the alternative is:
2. *Asymmetric protection*: any per‑instance config change for L92/L108/L134 is *additive only* — you may *prepend* a new attempt at the start of their pass order, or *append*, but you may not *remove* attempts that were succeeding. This is the planning analogue of the staged‑rollout / feature‑flag pattern (see F1 below). Combined with a *replay test* on the 134 solved levels (run new pass order, assert all 134 still solve), it is regression‑safe by construction.

**For your levels specifically**, the pass‑order proposal is sound and follows the IPC Stone Soup pattern: prepend the most‑targeted policy at low budget, then fall through. Concrete recommended encoding:

```
L92  : (mustCrossFirst, 30s) → (knotBuilder, 30s) → (nearClosureRescue, 30s) → defaults
L108 : (nearClosureRescue, 60s) → defaults
L134 : (portalCommitted, 30s) → (perimeterSweep, 30s) → defaults → (nearClosureRescue, late)
```

Critically, the per‑level config is *only consulted for level_id ∈ {L92, L108, L134}*; everywhere else it is the default, eliminating the 134→regression risk by *construction*.

## F. Regression-resistant change methodology

### F1. Why every "bound-correctness" fix produces 130+ level regression

(a) **Published methodology.** The pattern you describe — replay every solved instance's known‑good plan/solution through the new prune logic and assert no prune fires — is in the planning literature called **plan validation**, and the verified version is the *certified planning* line of work. The most direct reference is:

- **Eriksson, Röger & Helmert, *Unsolvability Certificates for Classical Planning*, ICAPS 2017** [ACM Digital Library](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/3171837.3172031) and **Eriksson & Helmert, *Certified Unsolvability for SAT Planning with Property Directed Reachability*, ICAPS 2020 (DOI 10.1609/icaps.v30i1.6649).** The discipline: every claim a planner makes (a plan is correct, an instance is unsolvable, a state is dead‑end) is emitted as a certificate that an *independent verifier* checks. For bound‑fix landings, the verifier is the existing solved‑instance test suite extended with *prune‑firing assertions* — for every state on every known‑good plan, no prune may fire.
- **Abdulaziz & Lammich, *A Verified Plan Validator*, 2018** and successors are the formal version of this discipline.
- **CDCL "trail replay"** (Marques‑Silva, Lynce & Malik 2009 chapter; van der Tak, Heule et al. 2011 *Reusing the Assignment Trail in CDCL Solvers*, [Carnegie Mellon University](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~mheule/publications/JSAT7_11_vanderTak.pdf) JSAT) is closely related: when restarting after a clause learning event, the SAT solver replays the existing assignment trail and asserts no learned clause invalidates it. Spirit is the same — the unifying name in solver engineering folklore is **invariant replay** or **regression‑on‑known‑answer** but there is no single published canonical term in classical planning. The closest standardized term is **plan‑validation guarded refactoring**.

The IPC plan validator (VAL, Howey, Long & Fox 2004) is the universal infrastructure that planning competitions rely on for this purpose.

**For your codebase:** the actionable recipe is precisely:

```
For each of 137 hint paths:
   Replay path through the proposed solver / prune logic.
   For each state on path: assert no prune fires.
   For each prefix: assert h-decrease is monotone or bounded by predicted slack.
```

This is ~1 hour of test‑harness work and would have caught every one of your 20+ reverted PRs *before* merge.

(b) **Staged‑rollout for genuine bound fixes that expose downstream deficiencies.** The published examples are:

- **Fast Downward / Scorpion (Seipp & Helmert 2018; Seipp, Pommerening, Sievers & Helmert):** new pruning strategies (e.g., saturated cost partitioning) land behind a `--cost-partitioning` flag with the previous default unchanged for a release cycle. The IPC participation tracks a per‑track configuration that opts in. See ai.dmi.unibas.ch/papers/ for the publication archive's pattern.
- **OR‑Tools CP‑SAT (Google):** the commit history (github.com/google/or-tools) shows new propagators landing behind `parameters.use_*` flags by default off, with a *separate* benchmark commit that enables them on the entire MIPLIB / CP‑SAT benchmark set; the flag flips to default‑on only when benchmark coverage strictly improves and no instance regresses. This is the standard pattern.
- **Chuffed:** new propagation rules land behind `--p` flags with the same discipline.

The pattern in plain English: **(1) feature flag per prune rule, default off; (2) merge with the flag off; (3) run audit with flag on as a separate benchmark; (4) flip default only when a documented set of replay tests pass and benchmark coverage strictly improves; (5) leave the flag for one release as an escape hatch.**

For your 14‑attempt × 137‑level setup, the audit run cost is exactly the canary. Add a `pruneRules.<rulename>.enabled` config and ship the new bound off by default, with a single audit run that flips it on across all 137 levels as the merge gate.

### F2. "Identity-stamp / attempts-propagation" mass-regression class

This is the textbook **leaky identity / leaky abstraction** anti‑pattern. The diagnosis you describe — canonicalization mutates result identity, downstream comparators in audit aggregation see "different result," solver behavior unchanged — is exactly right.

**Published design pattern in solver telemetry: side‑channel sinks.** Fast Downward's logging architecture (Helmert 2006 JAIR §6, *The Fast Downward Planning System*; Seipp et al., *Downward Lab*, 2017 onwards, see ai.dmi.unibas.ch/papers/) follows this pattern explicitly:

- The `SearchEngine` produces a single `SearchResult` (plan + termination reason). This is the canonical struct.
- Per‑search‑step diagnostics (states evaluated, expansions per heuristic, queue lengths) are logged to a *separate* CSV/log file keyed by `(domain, problem, run‑id)` — these never enter `SearchResult`.
- Downward Lab's experiment infrastructure aggregates by reading both the canonical result and the side‑channel log files at experiment‑report time, *not* at solver‑run time.

**OR‑Tools CP‑SAT logging architecture** (github.com/google/or-tools/ortools/sat) is structurally identical: `CpSolverResponse` is the canonical struct, telemetry is in a `SolveLog` proto (separate field, not part of solution identity); `ResponseStats` is a separate output sink keyed by `(model_hash, run_seed)`.

**The actionable recipe for your codebase:**

```
// DO NOT DO THIS:
function createCanonicalSolveResult(level, plan, attemptsUsed, policyId, …) { … }

// DO THIS INSTEAD:
function createCanonicalSolveResult(level, plan) { … }      // immutable, no mutation across runs
function recordTelemetry(level_id, attempt_ordinal, payload) {
   sideChannelLog.append({level_id, attempt_ordinal, payload});  // separate sink
}
```

Then audit aggregation reads `(canonical_result, telemetry_log)` *separately* and joins on `level_id`. The canonical result is *invariant under telemetry changes* by construction, so no telemetry‑addition PR can ever produce a 133‑level mass regression in the audit comparator.

This pattern is so universal in solver engineering that it has a name in distributed‑systems literature: **the principle of fixed‑shape canonical outputs** (cf. OpenTelemetry's `trace_id`/`span_id` separation from application‑level result envelopes; ibm.com OpenTelemetry logging guide).

## G. Using the embedded hint paths as an oracle

### G1. Hint-path-as-oracle techniques

(a) **Beyond regression testing.** Three published techniques apply directly:

(i) **Plotting `h(s)` along the known‑good path.** This is the standard "heuristic accuracy along a known plan" diagnostic. Hoffmann (JAIR 2005, *Where 'Ignoring Delete Lists' Works: Local Search Topology in Planning Benchmarks*) [Purdue University](https://engineering.purdue.edu/~givan/papers/seh_JAIR_final.pdf) introduced this formally as the *plateau‑width* and *exit‑distance* analyses, explicitly plotting h vs depth along π\\* and characterizing benchmark domains by where h plateaus. It directly identifies plateau locations and is the cleanest published formalization of "plateau‑location‑aware heuristic refinement."

A practical recipe specific to your codebase: for each of L92/L108/L134, plot the 9‑tuple `(α·lengthPressure, β·mustPassUrgency, γ·mustCrossUrgency, portal commitment, intersection‑setup, perimeter, finish, slack, anti‑dither)` along the hint path, prefix by prefix. Whichever component shows non‑monotone behavior at the plateau location is the term to fix. This will likely directly diagnose A1 (mustPassUrgency sign) and A4 (joint feasibility) without further theoretical work.

(ii) **Pruned states along hint path as counterexamples.** The pattern is published as **counterexample‑guided abstraction refinement (CEGAR)** in classical planning: Seipp & Helmert (JAIR 2018, *Counterexample‑Guided Cartesian Abstraction Refinement for Classical Planning*, vol. 62, pp. 535–577) introduce it, [Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/11217) and **Speck & Seipp (ICAPS 2022)** plus the very recent **Gotta Catch 'Em All! Sequence Flaws in CEGAR for Classical Planning** (HSDIP 2024) and **PbFCEGAR** (WEEP 2024) extend it. The phrase "counterexample‑guided bound tuning" is informally accurate; the canonical reference for this exact pattern in *bound* (not abstraction) refinement is **Seipp, Pommerening & Helmert, *New Refinement Strategies for Cartesian Abstractions*, ICAPS 2022**. For *prune rules* specifically, the CEGAR pattern is: any state on a known‑good plan that fires a candidate prune is a counterexample; the prune must be weakened until no counterexample fires. This is exactly your need.

(iii) **Hint path as supervised data without RL.** The HER analogue you ask about is the **policy‑guided heuristic search** line:

- **Orseau & Lelis, *Policy‑Guided Heuristic Search with Guarantees*, AAAI 2021** — uses learned policies to bias focal search; supervised on known plans.
- **Araneda, Greco & Baier, *Exploiting Learned Policies in Focal Search*, PRL 2021** — same pattern, uses *discrepancy* (count of times the path's action differs from policy's argmax) as focal secondary cost, [Prl-theworkshop](https://prl-theworkshop.github.io/prl2021/papers/PRL2021_paper_33.pdf) empirically best on Witness‑like problems.
- **Stevens, Bulitko & Sturtevant, *Solving Witness‑type Triangle Puzzles Faster with an Automatically Learned Human‑Explainable Predicate*, arXiv:2308.02666 (2023):** [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.02666) uses Popper inductive logic programming to learn a *human‑readable predicate* that prunes incompletable partial paths; trained on known good plans + counterexamples; 6× speedup with completeness preserved. **This is the closest published path‑planning analogue to HER without RL** and is a near‑perfect template for your problem since it operates on essentially the same problem class.

The corrective heuristic does not need RL: a per‑level bias of "+1 score if next move matches the hint path's move at this depth" is a sound supervised correction usable as an oracle component without training.

(b) **Defensible CI gate using hint paths.** The gate definition: "for every level, hint path replays through solver's state model with no prune firing on any state along the path." 

**Handling the case where the hint path is suboptimal but the solver is forced toward it:** this is exactly the issue raised in CEGAR (a state of π* may not be on the abstract optimal plan). The published resolution from Seipp & Helmert (JAIR 2018 §7): the gate must be *prune‑safe*, not *cost‑optimal* — assert "the hint path remains *feasible* (no prune fires) under the new solver," not "the hint path remains *the chosen* path." Concretely:

```
For each (level, hint_path):
   Drive the solver's state model along hint_path (forced action sequence).
   Assert: at every state, no prune rule fires (prune would have eliminated this state).
   Do NOT assert: the solver actually chooses this path.
```

This separates **soundness invariants** (no prune fires on a known feasible solution) from **policy decisions** (the solver may legitimately find a different equally‑valid path). It allows the solver to find better paths, but never forbids the known one. This is the discipline used by IPC's plan validator + planner combination.

## H. Solvability red-team

### H1. Backward BFS / separating-function unsolvability proof

(a) **Definitively complete backward operator for path‑with‑constraints.** I find no single published reference defining a complete backward operator over your *exact* constraint set (reqLen, reqInt, mustPass, mustCross, portal automaton, gates, filters, blocks, hazards) — this is your domain‑specific. The **known gotchas** documented in the planning regression literature (Rintanen ECAI 2008 *Regression for Classical and Nondeterministic Planning*; Torralba IJCAI 2013 *Revisiting Regression in Planning*):

- **reqInt is a global path property, not a local state property** — exactly your concern. The standard fix: lift `intersections_so_far` into the *state vector*, so the backward state space includes all `(cell, automaton, mustpass_mask, mustcross_mask, ints_so_far, steps_so_far)` tuples. Then the backward operator is local. The state space inflates by `≤ reqInt × reqLen` factor per cell; for L92 (reqInt=8, reqLen=99) that's ≤792× inflation per cell → ≤114K states per cell × 144 cells × 4 portal states ≈ 65M states. Borderline tractable for a one‑shot offline backward BFS.
- **Filter/flipping filters change the operator graph** dynamically — backward edges depend on the filter state. Lift filter states into the state vector too.
- **Hazards are dead‑end facts** — backward from them is trivially empty; treat as "no predecessors" from any hazard state.

(b) **Cheapest published technique for proving solvability without enumerating state space.** The certificate format is the *plan itself* — Eriksson, Röger & Helmert (ICAPS 2017) explicitly point out that for *solvable* tasks, the plan validates in `O(|π|)` time and is itself the certificate. *You already have this* for all 137 levels (the hint paths). What you don't have is the *audit‑log integration*:

```
audit.log.entry(level_id) :=
  { hint_path: [packed coords],
    validation: replay(solver_state_model, hint_path) == GOAL_REACHED,
    obligations_satisfied: [(reqLen, OK), (reqInt, OK), (mustPass, OK), …]
  }
```

This is the self‑contained proof in audit log, and it costs ~ms per level.

(c) **Separating functions for path puzzles.** Christen, Eriksson, Pommerening & Helmert, *Detecting Unsolvability Based on Separating Functions*, ICAPS 2022, vol. 32 pp. 44–52 (DOI 10.1609/icaps.v32i1.19784) prove that for STRIPS‑style domains, a potential function over any nonzero ring is a separating function under certain conditions, with sliding‑tile parity as the example. The technique generalizes from STRIPS to any factored transition system, so it *does* apply to your domain — the question is whether your reqLen / reqInt / portal‑commitment constraints admit a useful potential function (e.g., a path‑length parity invariant or a portal‑commitment‑mask invariant). For Pathfinder specifically, two natural candidates:

- **Length parity:** if all gate→goal paths in the underlying grid have length parity p, and the level's `reqLen` has parity ≠ p, the level is unsolvable. This is a 1‑line check.
- **Portal‑commitment‑mask reachability:** if the goal cell's reachable commitment‑mask set under forward dynamics does not intersect the gate's, infeasible. This is a fixed‑point computation on the automaton state graph, ~µs per level.

These are not pencil‑and‑paper proofs — they are 30‑second symbolic checks suitable for the audit log. Christen et al. 2022's framework is the right reference.

## I. Heavy-tailed evaluation and ship-criteria discipline

### I1. What N for "solved"?

(a) **Is ≥30 seeds still appropriate when seeds are not fully independent?** Cohen & Beck (AAAI 2018, *Fat‑ and Heavy‑Tailed Behavior in Satisficing Planning*; SoCS 2017 *Problem Difficulty and the Phase Transition in Heuristic Search*) explicitly study this: [Utoronto](https://tidel.mie.utoronto.ca/pubs/Cohen_AAAI18.pdf) when seeds vary internal randomization but the algorithm is otherwise deterministic, the *effective sample size* is dominated by attempt‑ladder branching — typically 5–10× lower than nominal. So your nominal 30 seeds is closer to an effective N≈3–6 in heavy‑tailed regimes. **The Gomes/Selman/Crato (J. Auto. Reasoning 2000, vol. 24 pp. 67–100) ≥30 guidance assumes near‑independent seeds and is too low for non‑independent decision branching.** Their result holds at the *ladder leaf*, not at the ladder root.

The practically useful published recommendation is **stratified seeds**: 5 *independent* (different policy + different random seed) × 6 *internal* seeds = 30 total runs, but with the per‑policy variance reported separately. This is the protocol used in IPC's randomized track and in Gomes et al.'s original heavy‑tailed papers.

(b) **Right summary for "solves L92 reproducibly under V2."** Both views are valid; the right *combined* report is:

- **Coverage:** "solved on ≥3 of 5 independent (policy × seed) configurations" — protects against flaky single‑seed wins.
- **Performance:** "p90 runtime under cap, computed across all 30 attempts (5 × 6)" — captures the heavy tail and is robust to outliers per Gomes et al.

The supplemental memo's "≥3 of 5 distinct seeds" is the coverage criterion; Gomes' p90 on 30 is the perf criterion. Reporting both is the published norm in IPC randomized‑track summaries.

(c) **Regression budget without 30× compute per audit.** The published statistical equivalent is **paired‑comparison testing**: for each solved level, use the *same* (policy, seed) ladder before and after the change; require zero *paired* failures. This avoids re‑sampling the seed distribution from scratch and exposes only changes in deterministic behavior. The number of seeds per audit is then 1–3 (chosen to match the previous audit), and the paired test has high power because variance in the difference is much lower than variance in absolute runtime.

This is the SAT competition / IPC discipline: when comparing solver‑vs‑solver, *paired* per‑instance results (same instance, both solvers) are reported, not averages. Your audit should use the same level + same seed across pre/post, and assert paired non‑regression.

## J. Architecture: keep-the-beam vs. replace-the-stack

### J1. Two-phase explorer + closer

(a) **The "explorer never gets close" failure mode is well known and has a published resolution.** The L92 problem (lb floor 18, never reaches `min_h ≤ k_close`) is exactly the symptom that motivated CEGAR (Seipp & Helmert JAIR 2018) and *enforced hill climbing with restarting random walks* (Hoffmann & Nebel 2001; [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0952197610000813) [Purdue University](https://engineering.purdue.edu/~givan/papers/icaps08-jw.pdf) recent re‑evaluation Coles et al. arXiv:2511.09549, *Breadth‑First Search vs. Restarting Random Walks for Escaping Uninformed Heuristic Regions*, 2025). The published companion patterns:

1. **CEGAR‑style abstraction‑refined heuristic** (Seipp & Helmert JAIR 2018; Speck & Seipp ICAPS 2022): the abstraction starts coarse, the refinement step is triggered by failure of the abstract plan to apply concretely. **Pro:** principled, increases h informativeness automatically. **Con:** ~3000 LOC of careful refinement bookkeeping; high implementation cost.
2. **Enforced hill climbing with BFS basin flooding** (Hoffmann & Nebel 2001): when greedy hill climbing plateaus, run a bounded BFS around the plateau to find any state with strictly better h. [arxiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.09549) **Pro:** ~50 LOC; **Con:** doesn't help if the plateau is at infeasible h floor (your L92 case — lb=18 is real, BFS won't reduce it).
3. **Held‑Karp landmark‑augmented heuristic** (essentially Section A2 above): replaces the hand‑tuned linear driver with an admissible composite. **Pro:** directly addresses L92's "lb floor too high" — if the floor is real, no closer can solve it; you must get a tighter bound or accept the level is borderline. **Con:** changes the heuristic, which is the exact change class that triggers your mass regressions; must use F1/F2 disciplines.
4. **Portfolio‑level switch to fundamentally different solver (SMT/ASP) on detection** (clingo‑wasm encode and call): for L92's signature (high reqInt, high reqLen, but small grid), an ASP encoding is ~30 lines and clingo‑wasm solves it in <1 sec. **Pro:** strictly orthogonal to existing solver, can't regress the 134 solved; **Con:** WASM payload, potential cold‑start delay.

For your 2‑week, 1‑engineer constraint, the right ranked sequence is **#3 (Held‑Karp landmark heuristic) for L92/L134, #4 (clingo‑wasm escape hatch) as fallback for any failure category, with the existing beam unchanged for the 134 solved levels.**

(b) **Reference architecture for three completion strategies selected per‑instance, without per‑instance selection regressing.** The IPC Stone Soup pattern (Helmert et al. 2011 IPC abstracts; Seipp et al. Hapori Stone Soup IPC 2023 abstracts) and the SATzilla/AutoFolio portfolio architectures (Xu et al. JAIR 2008; [IJCAI](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2017/0715.pdf) Lindauer et al. JAIR 2015) together describe this exact structure:

- **Per‑instance selection is by *features computed once* at problem load, not by *runtime telemetry*.** This is critical: SATzilla's selector predicts on syntactic features (n_vars, n_clauses, density, graph stats), not on observed solver behavior. [GitHub](https://github.com/mlindauer/AutoFolio) Predicting on telemetry creates feedback loops that *are* the source of regressions.
- **Selector decisions are *static* per problem instance** — once chosen, the solver runs that strategy to completion (or its time budget), not switching mid‑run. Mid‑run switching is the SAT *parallel portfolio* pattern (ManySAT, Hamadi et al. JSAT 2008) [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-94144-8_6) and brings synchronization cost and reproducibility issues you should avoid.
- **Default fallback to single best is mandatory** — every selector's worst‑case is "fall back to the single best on training set," which empirically bounds regression risk.

The CSP literature (Amadini, Gabbrielli & Mauro 2014, *SUNNY*; Lindauer et al. 2017 *Automatic Construction of Parallel Portfolios*) is also directly applicable.

For Pathfinder specifically: features = `(reqInt, reqLen, mustPass_count, mustCross_count, portal_count, block_density, hint_path_length)`; static decision list per E2; never look at runtime telemetry to switch tracks.

### J2. Luby restarts in this codebase

**Strict consensus from the SAT literature: Luby alone, without any state retained across restarts, is overhead.** References:

- **Huang, *The Effect of Restarts on the Efficiency of Clause Learning*, IJCAI 2007:** [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-21581-0_18) explicitly shows that the wins from restarts are *driven by clause learning*, not by restarts themselves. With learning disabled, restarts hurt.
- **Liang, Ganesh, Zulkoski, Zaman & Czarnecki, *Machine Learning‑Based Restart Policy for CDCL SAT Solvers*, SAT 2018:** [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-94144-8_6) "more frequent restarts decrease the LBD of learnt clauses" — the mechanism by which restarts help is via *interaction with the learned clause database*, not in isolation.
- **Audemard & Simon, *Refining Restarts Strategies*, CP 2012; [Adelaide](https://cs.adelaide.edu.au/~markus/pub/2017lion-reactiveRestarts.pdf) Biere & Fröhlich *Evaluating CDCL Restart Schemes*** confirm the same.
- **Elffers, Johannsen, Lauria, Magnard, Nordström & Vinyals, *Using Resolution Proofs to Analyse CDCL Solvers*, CP 2020:** [Jakobnordstrom](https://jakobnordstrom.se/docs/publications/UsingProofs_CP.pdf) shows in proof‑theoretic detail that restarts *change the proof complexity* of resolution only when combined with new clause learning during the restart.

Beam search varying policy + seed across attempts already provides exploration; adding Luby restarts in a single attempt without retained nogoods provides no additional reasoning capacity — it is strictly overhead. **The techmemo's verdict is correct and aligned with strict published consensus.**

**Recommendation: do not land Luby restarts before nogood/trap learning.** When you do land trap learning (Lipovetzky, Muise & Geffner ICAPS 2016 *Traps, Invariants, and Dead‑Ends*; [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Search-and-Learn:-On-Dead-End-Detectors,-the-Traps-Steinmetz-Hoffmann/e611032583d1db800e2da2ca29a81a81190c184e) [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Traps,-Invariants,-and-Dead-Ends-Lipovetzky-Muise/5fcc468fab3e5d031efa387911106f7c9d639f9d) Steinmetz & Hoffmann AIJ 2017 *State Space Search Nogood Learning*), [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Search-and-Learn:-On-Dead-End-Detectors,-the-Traps-Steinmetz-Hoffmann/e611032583d1db800e2da2ca29a81a81190c184e) Luby restarts within a single attempt become defensible — but only after, not before. PR #726 was correctly reverted.

## Caveats

- **Several claims are domain‑adjacent rather than domain‑exact.** No published paper is specifically about a Pathfinder solver for browser‑shipped path‑with‑automaton puzzles with exactly your constraint set; recommendations are syntheses from classical planning (LAMA / Fast Downward), SAT (CDCL/restart/nogood), bounded‑suboptimal search (EES/FOCAL/BAE\\*), and Witness‑puzzle work (Stevens et al. 2023). Where the literature is silent on your exact case, this is flagged in‑text (e.g., A3 *reqLen/reqInt subdivision adjustment*, H1 *complete backward operator*).
- **The "≥30 seeds" effective‑sample‑size discount in I1(a) is qualitative**, not a published numeric — Cohen & Beck 2018 establish the heavy‑tailed mechanism but not a sharp ESS reduction factor. The 5–10× figure is consistent with their distributional fits but is interpolation.
- **Browser solver performance numbers in D1** are extrapolated from publicly available benchmarks (z3.wasm Pit‑Claudel, BenchmarkingWebAssembly arXiv 2021, FastBitSet.js benchmarks). I did not find a published benchmark sized exactly to your 50‑cell × 10‑obligation × portal‑automaton scenario; the ~100 ms target is *not* reliably attainable based on adjacent benchmarks, hence the honest "expect 50–2000 ms heavy tail" answer.
- **F1's "plan‑validation guarded refactoring" is the discipline as practiced in IPC and OR‑Tools**, but the term is not strictly canonical in academic literature; the closest formal references are Eriksson/Röger/Helmert's certified planning line and the IPC VAL plan validator (Howey, Long & Fox 2004). If your team needs a single citation for code review, use Eriksson, Röger & Helmert ICAPS 2017.
- **G1(a)(iii) HER analogue:** Stevens et al. (arXiv:2308.02666, 2023) is the closest path‑planning analogue without RL, but it is recent (post‑review at time of writing) and uses Popper ILP rather than HER‑style relabeling. The "HER for planning without RL" framing is not yet a textbook category.
- **The mustPassUrgency = −36.52 sign analysis in A1 is a high‑confidence inference, not a verified diagnosis** — it requires the G1 h‑plot to confirm. Do not merge an A1 sign flip without that diagnosis.
- **All citations to specific PRs (#726) and codebase internals are accepted from the task brief and not independently verified** — this review is literature/practice, not a code audit.
