# Level Corpus Provenance and Overfitting Risk

## Purpose

This doc exists for one reason: **before trusting a claim that a solver/heuristic change
"improved" or "didn't regress" against some corpus of levels, know what that corpus actually is**.
Every corpus this project has — the published game, and both solver stress corpora — was built by
some process with its own biases, and several of them were built with explicit knowledge of the
solver's own weaknesses at the time. Tuning scoring weights, pruning rules, templates, or profiles
against a corpus that was itself shaped by the solver's past behavior risks a Goodhart's-law
feedback loop: the change measures "does the solver still handle the specific things we already
knew about," not "is the solver more robust in general." This doc is the reference for telling
those two things apart.

This is a risk-management reference, not a replacement for the corpora's own documentation
(`data/stress/README.md` for the stress corpora) — read this alongside that doc, not instead of it.

## 1. Published corpus (`data/levels.json`) — provenance and confidence

**Nothing about this is currently recorded on the level data itself** — `RawLevel`
(`modules/domain/level-schema.ts`) has no provenance field, and this information lives only in
this doc and in the separate Firestore-backed rating system. What follows is recorded here because
it exists nowhere else yet (Phase 2, see "Machine-readable phase" below, is the plan to fix that).

### Confidence tiers

- **Certainly human-authored:** level number > 130, unless the level is rated `garbage` (see
  below), in which case authorship is not asserted either way. **Also certainly human-authored:**
  any level (any number) rated `great`.
- **Certainly AI-generated:** any level rated `common`.
- **Uncertain, ~80% confidence AI-generated:** every other level — i.e. level number ≤ 130 and not
  rated `great`, `common`, or `garbage`. Generated by one of 5 different AI models, in sporadic
  batches across roughly an 8-month span. Exact per-level model/batch attribution is **not**
  reconstructable from anything currently recorded — do not treat any more specific attribution
  than "uncertain, ~80% AI" as established fact for this tier.

`garbage`, `common`, and `great` are existing predefined rating tags (`index.html`'s rating button
list), not something invented for this doc.

### What the AI-generated majority actually looks like (the part that matters for overfitting risk)

These levels were **not designed against any particular difficulty criteria**. They typically
showcase one or two grid objects, in limited quantities, and were **never designed to be
deliberately antagonistic to the solver**. Where they're difficult at all, that difficulty is
incidental to whatever the generating model happened to produce — not engineered the way stress
corpus 1's batch A/E levels were (see below). That makes this population a meaningfully different
kind of evidence: a solver change that only helps on stress-corpus-1's deliberately-adversarial
levels, but not here, is a much weaker generalization signal than one that helps here too, because
this corpus was never shaped by anyone's model of the solver's weaknesses.

### Mechanical note: where to actually get the tags

Ratings live in Firestore's `level_ratings` collection, keyed by level **fingerprint** (not level
number) — see `modules/persistence/level-rating-repository.ts` and
`modules/engine/level-rating-manager.ts` (fingerprint lookup + legacy-fingerprint fallback).
Export a joinable snapshot with `npm run levels:ratings-report -- --json`
(`scripts/level-ratings-report.mjs`), which produces a flat array with `levelNumber`, `tags`, and
`customTags` — the same shape `scripts/hint-corpus-expand.mjs`'s `--ratings=<path>
--skip-tags=garbage` already consumes (see `docs/archive/hint-corpus-expansion-plan.md` for that
existing consumer).

## 2. Stress corpus 1 — hypothesis-driven levels (`S001`–`S150`)

Full detail lives in `data/stress/README.md`'s "Batches" section — this section only adds the
overfitting-risk framing on top of it.

These 150 levels were generated to deliberately stress-test the solver **as it existed at
generation time**: 113 of 150 initially failed to solve; the solver was then iteratively hardened
until all 150 passed. That has a direct consequence for how to read a "passes all 150" claim today:
it mostly certifies **"the specific weaknesses these levels were built to expose, as of that past
solver state, are now fixed"** — not "the solver is robust against novel adversarial construction
in general." The six batches carry very different amounts of this risk:

| Batch | Theory | Overfitting risk if reused later |
|---|---|---|
| A `historical-solver-pain` | Ridge model fitted on historical solver audit logs steers generation toward feature regimes that were historically slow | **High** — literally trained on the solver's own failure history |
| E `anti-heuristic` | Directly opposes named mechanisms in `solver/attempts.ts` (near-closure rule, perimeter-led ordering, the `reqLen≥90` multi-gate budget floor, flipper diverse-beam-ladder bait, navDensity-threshold gaming) | **High** — if any of those named mechanisms are later replaced or refactored, this batch's difficulty may go stale (it stops testing anything real) rather than continuing to measure general robustness |
| B `structural-complexity`, D `novel-topology` | Maximize mechanic interaction / novel witness geometry, independent of solver internals | Lower — not reasoned from the solver's own weaknesses |
| C `deceptive-simplicity`, F `wild-witness` | Ambiguity from open geometry / maximally wide, unhypothesized parameter draws | Lowest — closest in spirit to "unbiased," though still solver-aware in that a human picked "ambiguity" and "wide coverage" as the axes worth generating for |

`stressMeta.predictionConfidence` (per level) already encodes a rough trust level along a different
axis (lowest for D/F, highest for A, per the existing batch table) — read it alongside pass-rate
analysis, but note it's about *predicted difficulty accuracy*, not about *adversarial-staleness
risk* — the two are related but not the same thing, and this doc's risk column above is the one
relevant to "should I trust a heuristic-tuning result validated against this batch."

**No static filters were used in generation** (explicit choice, both stress corpora — see
`data/stress/README.md`'s "Guarantees"). Neither stress corpus provides *any* evidence about static
filter behavior. A "verified against the stress corpus, no regression" claim says nothing about
static filters — validate those against the published corpus instead, which does contain them.

## 3. Stress corpus 1's migrated random levels (`R####`) and stress corpus 2 (unsolved subset)

`data/stress/README.md`'s "Second corpus" section already contains the canonical statement of why
this corpus exists and the exact risk it's designed to avoid — read it there; it's better than
anything restated here. The short version: corpus 1's batches were deliberately hypothesis-driven
(reasoned from the solver's own strategy), which is "exactly what makes it risky as the *only*
stress corpus once the solver has been iterated against it repeatedly" (the doc's own words) — so
this second corpus was generated **solver-blind**: no import of `solver/attempts.ts`, `policy.ts`,
`archetype.ts`, or audit history at any point in generation.

That solver-blindness is real, but it isn't the same thing as "representative of typical levels."
The generation brief imposed its own biases, all in the direction of *harder and denser*, never
*smaller or sparser*:

- Grids **11×11 to 15×15 only** — never smaller, unlike corpus 1's batch F which went as small as
  4×4.
- Every mechanic present is drawn from the **upper ~45% of its range** when present at all (e.g.
  present → 5–8 of a max-8 range, never 1–4) — a coin-flip on presence, then skewed high on count.
  Object caps were also raised roughly **+4 above the game's documented maximums** for this
  generation specifically.
- **Single gate, single goal only** — corpus 1's batch E used multiple gates specifically as a
  budget-starvation attack tool, so this corpus avoids them to not reintroduce solver-targeted
  design through the back door.
- **No static filters** (same exclusion as corpus 1).

So: passing this corpus is real evidence against solver failure on **large, dense, object-rich,
single-gate** levels specifically — it is not a general robustness certificate, and it says nothing
about small levels, sparse levels, multi-gate levels, or static filters. The 300 levels that turned
out to be immediately solvable were migrated into corpus 1 (now living at `id` prefix `R####`,
`stressMeta.corpusName: "random-uniform-v1"`, `generationBatch: null` — they carry corpus 2's
solver-blind provenance despite now living in the corpus-1 file). The remaining 1700
(all currently unsolved/timeout) stay in `stress-levels-random.json`.

## 4. Using this to validate a solver/heuristic change without overfitting

Before trusting "change X improved pass rate / didn't regress against corpus Y":

1. **Which corpus, specifically?** Published (mostly incidental difficulty, not adversarial),
   stress-1 batch A/E (deliberately adversarial to a *past* solver state), stress-1 batch
   B/C/D/F (adversarial in spirit but not solver-reasoned), or the solver-blind
   random-migrated/stress-2 levels (blind, but grid-size/density/object-count biased)?
2. **If batch A or E specifically:** does the change touch the same mechanism the batch was built
   to defeat? If so, "improvement" may just mean "we finally patched the thing this level was built
   to expose," not a generalizable gain — check whether the change also holds up on the
   solver-blind corpora before calling it a general robustness improvement.
3. **Does the claim depend on static filters?** Neither stress corpus contains any — validate
   against the published corpus instead/also.
4. **Does the claim depend on small or sparse levels?** The solver-blind random corpus never
   generated below 11×11 nor at low object-density — validate against stress-1's non-random batches
   or the published corpus instead.
5. **Cross-corpus agreement is the actual signal.** A change that only improves stress-corpus-1
   pass rate, without also holding on the published corpus and the solver-blind random corpus, is
   the highest-risk pattern for "fixed a specific known adversarial case" rather than "generalized."
   A change that holds across all three kinds of corpus (incidental/AI-authored, historically
   solver-aware-adversarial, and solver-blind-but-density-biased) is much stronger evidence.

## Machine-readable phase (planned, not yet built)

This doc is deliberately narrative-first, per explicit instruction. A follow-up phase should make
the biases and coverage gaps above legible to tooling — `solver:bench`, `stress:analyze`, and the
`scripts/analyze-*` family — so a report can automatically annotate a result with the relevant
caveat (e.g. "this batch excludes static filters" or "this batch is solver-aware-adversarial")
instead of relying on a human remembering to read this doc first. Candidate hooks, not yet
implemented:

- A corpus/batch-level `coverageGaps: string[]` and `adversarialIntent: 'solver-aware' |
  'solver-blind' | 'incidental'` field, alongside the existing per-level `stressMeta` (same
  sibling-object pattern the stress corpora already use — not a change to the validated `RawLevel`
  schema).
- A published-corpus provenance annotation capturing the confidence tiers in section 1 above,
  again as a sibling structure rather than a new `RawLevel` field, keyed by level number (or
  fingerprint, to survive reordering) with an explicit confidence enum rather than a boolean, so
  the ~80%-confidence tier can never be silently read as certain.

See `docs/hint-workbench-implementation-plan.md` for an example of how a prior "doc first, tooling
second" plan was tracked to completion, if this becomes a formal follow-up plan.
